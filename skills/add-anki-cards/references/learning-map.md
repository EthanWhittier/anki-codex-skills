# Learning maps: breadth, depth, and durable practice

Use for chapter/topic coverage, a substantial deck-building project, or a staged learning sequence. A single requested card does not require a new map. Reuse an existing map and its location; when `study-section` is active, extend its unit ledger instead of creating a competing tracker. AI maintains the map; the learner reads, reasons, chooses priorities, and practices.

## Four levels of learning

| Level | Capability | Useful targets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Terminology and foundations | Recall and interpret the language and essential building blocks. | Terms with meaning, notation, important facts, rules, conditions, conventional associations. |
| 2. Basic concepts and use | Explain the idea, recognize instances, and use it in a straightforward case. | Examples/non-examples, diagrams, classification, simple distinctions, direct applications. |
| 3. Depth and discrimination | Explain why, distinguish nearby cases, and reason about conditions and changes. | Mechanisms, meaningful comparisons, error diagnosis, predictions, limitations, counterexamples. |
| 4. Synthesis and transfer | Select and combine ideas in a problem or context beyond the rehearsed example. | Multi-concept reasoning, worked deductions, cross-section connections, novel cases, authentic tasks. |

This is a planning framework, not a universal scientific hierarchy or four compulsory cards per concept. Levels overlap. Establish enough foundations for the next task, while using examples and applications to deepen those foundations. Do not wait for perfect recall of every term before reasoning, or demand advanced mastery before retaining a useful definition or rule. Basic meaning and relevant conditions should be understood; deeper explanation can develop later. Retain useful foundational cards when advanced cards are added.

**Learning depth and card format are separate choices.** A fixed question can require deep synthesis; a generated problem can practice elementary recognition. Choose fixed-answer, fixed open-response, or generated-problem format for the target, not as an automatic promotion ladder. Read the generative-blueprints reference when actually designing those exercise types.

## Adapt to the discipline and source

Apply the framework across topics and textbooks without importing Logic's content, number of stages within a level, card counts, or proof-centered tasks. Let the actual source, learner goals, and discipline determine important knowledge and what counts as an adequate response. For example:

- In science, foundations and diagrams can support mechanisms, prediction, interpretation of evidence, and observation or experiment.
- In history, people, terms, chronology, and context can support causal explanation, source evaluation, comparison, and qualified arguments.
- In literature or philosophy, vocabulary and accurate comprehension can support close reading, argument reconstruction, objections, and evidence-supported interpretations.
- In programming or mathematics, notation and rules can support examples, diagnosis, derivations, implementation, and unfamiliar problems.
- For practical, perceptual, or creative skills, include actual performance, observation, making, or feedback beyond what cards can establish.

These are illustrations, not required palettes. Do not force every subject into a mechanism/proof exercise or every narrative into a mastery course. Distinguish factual recall from interpretation, convention, approximation, and contested claims. For open judgments, require relevant evidence and a defensible argument rather than agreement with an AI exemplar; preserve uncertainty and source limitations. Planning should use the available textbook's structure and verified content, not a generic list presented as that book's coverage.

## Build and reuse a source-grounded map

1. Establish the unit/source and desired scope. Distinguish selected mastery goals, context-only reading, and reference/skip material. Comprehensive coverage means the important knowledge and capabilities within that chosen scope, not every sentence or every possible extension.
2. Inspect the source's explanations, terminology, figures, worked examples, and relevant exercises. Identify core topic clusters, prerequisites, and meaningful outcomes across the four levels. A few high-level milestones can contain many supporting targets; milestone count is not card count. Mark unsupported additions as extensions.
3. Reconcile with prior evidence and existing cards where available. If live access fails, label any dated snapshot and leave current coverage unverified. An existing advanced card supports its explicit task, not automatically every definition or fact mentioned in its answer. Conversely, genuinely established knowledge need not receive redundant cards.
4. Propose the map and coverage priorities before a total card estimate. Show the learner a compact overview, inviting corrections and personal connections; do not require them to invent the curriculum or approve every row. Record accepted scope and exclusions. Existing authorization and the card skill's exact-approval rules still apply.
5. Work from consequential gaps in the map instead of inventing the next topic ad hoc. Cover neglected foundations before endlessly deepening a favorite concept, while allowing a useful higher-level application when its prerequisites are ready.

### Foundation and dependency audit

Before estimating cards or treating the map as sufficiently complete, perform three checks:

1. **Source inventory:** Inventory the source's important terms, entities, families, distinctions, mechanisms, and applications across its prose, headings, figures, tables, worked examples, exercises, summary, and review questions. Give each important item a deliberate master, context, or reference/skip disposition. Do not turn the inventory into a card list.
2. **Dependency backtrace:** For every basic-concept, depth, or synthesis target, identify the terminology, objects, rules, and distinctions it presupposes. A prerequisite materially introduced or explained in the selected unit needs its own map row or an explicit disposition. Do not infer that the learner understands every named object in a relationship merely because they reasoned correctly about that relationship.
3. **Coverage reconciliation:** For each chosen target, record whether support is a verified card, an approved or unsaved candidate, chosen non-card practice, established knowledge needing no new card, deferred work, or a deliberate omission. Require a distinct retrieval purpose before allocating a card; mapping an important foundation does not itself require memorization work.

When starting mid-chapter or mid-course, distinguish assumed prerequisites from material first substantively taught in the selected unit. Mark assumed familiarity as unverified unless prior evidence supports it, and inspect only the earlier source material needed to understand the dependency. If the learner later reports that a mapped prerequisite is unfamiliar, update its evidence and priority, teach it directly when useful, and reconsider dependent cards without treating the discovery as a reason for automatic deck expansion.

Use a compact table or nested checklist with stable topic/outcome IDs. Record topic/source, intended capability/level, coverage support, learning evidence, and next action. Group rows by topic where that keeps the record readable. Store only enough detail to resume and audit omissions; do not duplicate the textbook.

Keep these separate:

- **Coverage support:** planned, supported by verified note IDs or chosen non-card practice, deliberately no new card, deferred, or out of scope. Distinguish an unsaved candidate from a verified card.
- **Learning evidence:** not assessed, practicing with help, demonstrated now, demonstrated later, and whether a novel application was attempted successfully. Note assistance and the limits of the observation. Card existence or a checked coverage box is not evidence of mastery.
- **Reading position:** what the learner reports reading, independently of coverage or learning evidence.

Unchecked planned coverage is a visible design gap, not a compulsory block on reading. Optional ideas create no homework until chosen. Preserve scope changes and prior evidence when the map is revised.

## Coverage and workload

Estimate cards from useful distinct retrieval targets and existing coverage. There is no default section-wide cap, required level ratio, or target of minimizing card count. Minimum information applies to each ordinary card's focus, not to how little of the subject deserves practice. A meaningful terminology or fact target need not justify itself through an advanced mechanism or predicted mistake.

The one-anchor-plus-contrast heuristic is for a simple or low-priority topic, not a multi-concept chapter or section. Avoid both exhaustive automatic card generation and packing independent targets into a few overloaded cards. A coherent synthesis can stay together when the task and review protocol justify it.

Distinguish the estimated total coverage from the next authoring batch. Explain meaningful omissions and deferrals, and agree on substantial expansions under the main skill's budget rules. When time is short, save the remaining chosen targets as the next action rather than silently narrowing the learning goal. Balance pace against observed review time, backlog, difficulty, and learner preference; do not invent workload estimates or scheduling changes from card count alone. Keep foundational practice, problem-solving, and reading all represented in the time budget.

## Authoring, synthesis, and later practice

Use the learner's existing explanation when adequate. Otherwise offer a candidate front and let them supply the meaning-bearing answer; help directly when needed. Do not force the learner to rediscover a conventional term or rule. Verify technical meaning and preserve personal wording where correct. Record the verified card IDs against the targets they actually support.

At natural boundaries or when a connection becomes useful, offer one focused synthesis probe. Sometimes let the learner choose the connection; sometimes name the relevant ideas and leave the reasoning to them. Verify the connection and its limits. A worthwhile understood connection may become a card through the normal authorship process. Do not wait for synthesis before adding useful section cards, and do not manufacture cross-subject connections or require every pair of topics to be linked.

For later practice, combine spaced recall with selected fresh applications that test the intended capability. Use Anki's scheduled review for existing cards; avoid duplicating that work with an unnecessary parallel quiz queue. Broader applications may live in Anki or ordinary exercises. A generation blueprint represents a bounded, validated exercise family; varying names alone does not establish transfer.

Use worked examples or hints when prerequisites are weak, then reduce support and check what the learner can do independently. Mix relevant, confusable cases when selecting the right idea is itself a goal. Assess after meaningful gaps as well as immediately, without claiming permanent mastery from one success. If difficulty reveals a missing prerequisite, repair that dependency; do not respond automatically with more cards or harder problems.
